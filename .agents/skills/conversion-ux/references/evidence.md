# Evidence behind the rules

The source videos ([sources.md](sources.md)) present every before-and-after as a win, and none shows data. This file separates what published research supports from what the presenters assert. Read the entry before citing a study in a finding, and cite the paper, not the video.

Citations were checked on 2026-10-24 against the papers or their publisher pages. Where only a secondary source confirmed a detail, the entry says so.

## Studies the videos cite

### Choice overload (the jam study)

Video: a Columbia study found 3% of people bought from 24 jams and 30% from 6 (2TlIg3VokY8 0:43).

Paper: Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing? *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 79(6), 995-1006. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995. Iyengar was at Columbia, Lepper at Stanford; the tasting table was in a grocery store (Draeger's, Menlo Park, per secondary sources).

What it found: 60% of passers-by stopped at the 24-jam table against 40% at the 6-jam table. Of those who stopped, about 3% bought with 24 jams against about 30% with 6. The video drops the first half: the larger display attracted more people.

Later work: Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 37(3), 409-425 (50 experiments, N = 5,036) found a mean effect of "virtually zero" with large variance. Chernev, A., Böckenholt, U., & Goodman, J. (2015). Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis. *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 25(2), 333-358, found the effect appears under four conditions: complex choice sets, difficult decision tasks, uncertain preferences, and a goal of picking the single best option.

Use: cut or structure options when the options are hard to compare or the user does not know what they want (rule CH2). Do not claim fewer options always convert better.

### Defaults

Video: "70 to 90% of users in most products never change the default values" (2TlIg3VokY8 1:12).

No primary source found for that range. Treat it as unsourced. Related, smaller and informal: Jared Spool (UIE, 2011) reported that under 5% of a sample of early Microsoft Word users had changed any setting.

Research on defaults themselves is strong: Johnson, E. J., & Goldstein, D. G. (2003). Do defaults save lives? *Science*, 302(5649), 1338-1339 (online experiment: 42% agreed to be donors under opt-in, 82% under opt-out). Madrian, B. C., & Shea, D. F. (2001). The power of suggestion: Inertia in 401(k) participation and savings behavior. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 116(4), 1149-1187 (participation rose sharply under automatic enrollment). Status quo bias: Samuelson, W., & Zeckhauser, R. (1988). Status quo bias in decision making. *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty*, 1(1), 7-59.

Use: defaults change behaviour a lot, which is why rule CH1 and honesty item HO4 forbid defaults that add cost or consent.

### Endowed progress (the car-wash card)

Video: Columbia researchers ran the car-wash card study; the head start nearly doubled completion; called the goal-gradient effect (2TlIg3VokY8 1:45).

Paper: Nunes, J. C., & Drèze, X. (2006). The endowed progress effect: How artificial advancement increases effort. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 32(4), 504-512. doi:10.1086/500480. Nunes was at USC and Drèze at Wharton, not Columbia. 300 cards: 34% of the 10-stamp cards with 2 stamps pre-filled were completed against 19% of the plain 8-stamp cards.

The video's corrections: the effect's name is the endowed progress effect, and the car-wash cards gave a reason for the free stamps (a sign-up promotion). In the paper's survey study, a head start counted in purchases with no reason given had no effect; any stated reason, even a thin one, restored it. The goal-gradient name belongs to a related Columbia paper: Kivetz, R., Urminsky, O., & Zheng, Y. (2006). The goal-gradient hypothesis resurrected: Purchase acceleration, illusionary goal progress, and customer retention. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 43(1), 39-58 (café card: a 12-stamp card with 2 bonus stamps was completed faster than a 10-stamp card).

Use: start progress above zero by counting a step the user really completed, and say why it counts. The video's closing line that "even a fake" progress works is rejected (rule GA4).

### Reciprocity

Video: Cialdini ranked reciprocity "the single most powerful driver of human behavior"; free samples increase purchases "by up to 2,000%" (2TlIg3VokY8 5:03-5:16).

Cialdini, R. B. *Influence* (first edition 1984) calls reciprocity "one of the most potent" weapons of influence; it comes first in chapter order but is not ranked above the others. Classic experiment: Regan, D. T. (1971). Effects of a favor and liking on compliance. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 7(6), 627-639 (people given an unasked-for soft drink bought about twice as many raffle tickets).

The 2,000% figure traces to Pinsker, J. "The Psychology Behind Costco's Free Samples", *The Atlantic*, 1 October 2014, which links it to a trade-press item about a grocery chain (Marsh), with no study behind it. The same article quotes Costco's sampling contractor for +71% (beer) and +600% (frozen pizza), also without published data. Drop the 2,000% number.

Use: give a real, useful partial result before asking for an account (rule GA2). No percentage lift should be promised.

### IKEA and endowment effects

Video: people value what they build more; Duolingo has users finish a lesson before signing up (2TlIg3VokY8 5:36-7:03).

Paper: Norton, M. I., Mochon, D., & Ariely, D. (2012). The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love. *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 22(3), 453-460. Four studies (IKEA boxes, origami, Lego). The video leaves out the limit: when participants built and then destroyed their creations, or failed to finish them, the effect disappeared. Endowment effect: Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L., & Thaler, R. H. (1990). Experimental tests of the endowment effect and the Coase theorem. *Journal of Political Economy*, 98(6), 1325-1348.

Use: the build-before-sign-up step must be short enough to finish, and what the user made must survive sign-up (rule GA3).

### Loss aversion

Video: Kahneman won the Nobel for proving losses are "twice as powerful" as gains; threats beat pitches "every time" (2TlIg3VokY8 8:27-9:21).

Papers: Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk. *Econometrica*, 47(2), 263-291. Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1992). Advances in prospect theory: Cumulative representation of uncertainty. *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty*, 5(4), 297-323, where the often-quoted 2.25 is a median from 25 graduate students. Kahneman shared the 2002 economics prize with Vernon Smith; Tversky had died in 1996.

Later work: Brown, A. L., Imai, T., Vieider, F. M., & Camerer, C. F. (2024). Meta-analysis of empirical estimates of loss aversion. *Journal of Economic Literature*, 62(2), 485-516 (607 estimates, mean about 1.96, so roughly twice holds on average, with wide spread). Gal, D., & Rucker, D. D. (2018). The loss of loss aversion: Will it loom larger than its gain? *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 28(3), 497-516, argue losses do not generally outweigh gains; it was published with replies, including Simonson and Kivetz (2018). Yechiam, E., & Zeif, D. (2025). Loss aversion is not robust: A re-meta-analysis. *Journal of Economic Psychology*, 107, 102801, reanalysed the Brown et al. data: strong loss aversion appeared when losses were smaller than gains or the outcomes were shown in order, and about 1.07 (not significantly above 1) when gains and losses were symmetric and unordered. The size of the effect depends on how the choice is presented. The video's "every time" is not supported, and the studies concern money gambles, not upgrade prompts.

Use: name real losses of the user's own things, plainly, with dates (rule EX2). The video's "I'll risk it" dismiss copy is confirmshaming and is excluded.

### Anchoring, contrast and decoys

Video: ranges are read at the high end; a struck-through price makes the current one feel like a deal; a $50 fee feels small beside a $1,900 laptop; a $90 steak makes the $40 salmon look reasonable (zr37ibqXl1U 7:38, 12:47; 2TlIg3VokY8 9:36-10:51).

Anchoring: Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. *Science*, 185(4157), 1124-1131. Decoy (attraction) effect: Huber, J., Payne, J. W., & Puto, C. (1982). Adding asymmetrically dominated alternatives: Violations of regularity and the similarity hypothesis. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 9(1), 90-98.

The specific claim that people read a price range at its upper end is the presenter's; no study is named. The laptop example compares a monthly fee with a one-off price ("$49/month · just 2.6%"); per year the plan costs about 31% of the laptop. Reference prices are regulated in some markets: in the EU, a price reduction on goods must show the lowest price of the previous 30 days as the earlier price (Directive 98/6/EC, Article 6a, added by Directive (EU) 2019/2161). The Commission's guidance notice on Article 6a (Official Journal C 526, 29 December 2021, section 1.1) says the directive covers goods, not services or digital content, and member states may set exceptions.

Use: anchors and context are real effects, so the rules only allow true reference prices and like-for-like comparisons (UN12, UN13).

### Named effects with no matching research term

- **"Transparency bias"** (zr37ibqXl1U 3:10): not an established term. The nearest research is on two-sided messages, where admitting a downside can raise credibility depending on conditions: Eisend, M. (2006). Two-sided advertising: A meta-analysis. *International Journal of Research in Marketing*, 23(2), 187-198. Also related: Buell, R. W., & Norton, M. I. (2011). The labor illusion: How operational transparency increases perceived value. *Management Science*, 57(9), 1564-1579. ("Illusion of transparency" in psychology means something unrelated.)
- **"Evaluative ease"** (zr37ibqXl1U 8:39): not an established term. The related research is evaluability: Hsee, C. K. (1996). The evaluability hypothesis: An explanation for preference reversals between joint and separate evaluations of alternatives. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 67(3), 247-257.
- **"Commitment consistency"** (zr37ibqXl1U 9:20): Cialdini's commitment and consistency principle, a real concept; the destination-card example is the presenter's.

## Presenter claims with no study

Everything below is a design opinion, sometimes a good one, with no data shown. Label findings that rest on them "Presenter":

- Every "B converts better" or "dramatically more bookings" result in zr37ibqXl1U.
- "Start my free trial" beats "Subscribe and start 7 days for free"; "my" creates ownership.
- A number ("2 taps", "23 minutes") beats an adjective.
- Sensory titles and large photos raise bookings.
- Counts on filter options, histograms and mortgage helpers help people decide (U7jTCgIWS4w).
- Above-the-fold cramming is outdated because everyone scrolls.

Visual craft rules from GGg61sdEjeI and U7jTCgIWS4w (grid, type, colour, shape language, icons) are conventions judged by inspection; the videos make no conversion claim for most of them.

## How to talk about evidence in a finding

- Research: name the paper and the condition under which it holds.
- Adjacent research: name the paper and its setting (gambles, raffle tickets, loyalty cards), and present the rule as a reason to test.
- Presenter: say it is a heuristic and suggest the test.
- Never quote a lift percentage from the videos.
