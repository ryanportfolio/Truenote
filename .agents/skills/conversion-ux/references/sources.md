## zr37ibqXl1U: three A/B pairs (paywall, ride hailing, booking)

Thesis (14:00): every element on a screen asks the user a question, and that question decides whether they act or hesitate.

**Paywall, $19/month with a 7-day trial (0:36-5:20).**
- A: space-art hero, headline "Get access to 1,000+ games", price, three feature bullets, button "Subscribe and start 7 days for free". The presenter says this makes a new user answer "Is this worth $19 a month?" before they have used the product, a judgement most people postpone forever (1:32-2:02).
- B: headline "How your free trial works". The question becomes "Can I try this for free?", which is easy to answer yes (2:05-2:28).
- B replaces bullets with a three-step timeline: Today you unlock the library; Day 5 we remind you the trial is ending; Day 7 the first charge, cancel any time before (2:31-2:50).
- The Day 5 reminder line is presented as the strongest element: the company volunteers the downside, which the presenter calls "transparency bias" (2:51-3:20). Frames show two pills: "We're trying to trap you" (grey) vs "We're going to remind you" (purple).
- Button: "Start my free trial". "Subscribe" reads as a recurring commitment; "Start" as a beginning; "my" as ownership (3:23-3:55).
- Under the button: "Start in 2 taps", which answers "is there a form, do I need a card, how many steps" with a number. Same logic: "delivery in 23 minutes" beats "fast delivery" (3:56-4:20).
- Hero shows actual game characters instead of decorative art: users cannot commit to what they cannot picture (4:20-4:44).
- Summary line: the best paywall makes the user feel safe, not sold (5:16).

**Ride hailing, same route and similar average fare (6:53-10:36).**
- A shows price ranges per car type ($13-17, $17-22, $16-21). The presenter claims people read a range as its high end, so three ranges become three worst cases and the easiest choice is to leave (7:38-8:23).
- B shows one number per option. Called "evaluative ease" in the video (8:30-8:43).
- B adds the pickup time ("12:53 pm, 2 minutes away"), turning a cost decision into a convenience one (8:45-8:58).
- A green one-word "Cheaper" badge on one option (8:58-9:12). The video notes it never says cheaper than what.
- A destination card already on the map: the user has decided where; the remaining question is which car. Called commitment and consistency (9:13-9:31).
- Closing claim: a range where a number is possible creates doubt (9:31-9:38).

**Booking, same villa at €89 a night (10:37-13:56).**
- A is a correct, plain form: small thumbnail, "Beach house with garden", rating, price, two date inputs, guests, "Reserve" (11:12-11:52).
- B: the photo takes the top half with badges (Superhost, Guest favourite, 1/24 photos). "A informs, B transports" (11:54-12:18).
- Title "Beachside escape steps from the sand": sensory and specific about distance (12:18-12:46).
- Price €129 struck through, €89, green "-31%" badge (12:47-13:05).
- Dates as "Friday, March 28 to Wednesday, April 2" with a "5 nights" badge, so the user does no date math (13:06-13:28).
- Button "Reserve · €445 total"; under it a green shield line "Free cancellation before March 26", answering the most common booking objection before it is asked (13:28-13:55).

## 2TlIg3VokY8: six psychology principles

1. **Smart defaults (0:20-1:45).** A booking form with five empty fields vs the same fields pre-filled with the most common choices and a button "Search 12 Available Tables". Cites the jam study and an unsourced "70 to 90% of users never change defaults". Rule: pre-select the most common choice; the task becomes scan and adjust.
2. **Goal gradient (1:45-3:13).** Cites the car-wash card study (attributed in the video to Columbia; see evidence.md). Onboarding at 0% with five empty stamps vs the same flow at 20% with account creation counted as step 1. LinkedIn's profile-strength meter never starts at zero. Frames show a "You're doing great!" header, five step dots and a fire icon by the percentage.
3. **Reciprocity (3:49-5:36).** An SEO scan whose results are blurred behind "Create an account to see your report" vs one that shows the score, top issues and passes, then offers "Save My Report". Cites Cialdini and an unsourced "free samples increase purchases by up to 2,000%". Examples: Costco samples, Spotify's premium trial, Notion's free tier.
4. **IKEA and endowment effects (5:36-7:03).** Email, password, sign up vs a flow where the user picks a name, title, palette and card style before signing up, with a "Continue" button. Duolingo has users pick a language, set a goal and finish a lesson before the account screen.
5. **Loss aversion (8:19-9:36).** "Upgrade to Premium" with "Maybe later" vs "Your Files Will Be Deleted": storage full, oldest files deleted in 3 days, named files at risk, "Total: 2.8 GB will be lost", button "Protect My Files Now", dismiss "I'll Risk It". The video calls the second a threat and says threats win. The dismiss copy is confirmshaming; see the honesty guard in SKILL.md.
6. **Contrast effect (9:36-11:00).** A $49.99/month protection plan alone (the video multiplies it to $599.88/year) vs the same plan under a $1,899 laptop at checkout with "Just 2.6%". Frames show "$49/month" next to the one-off laptop price, so the percentage compares a monthly fee with a one-off price. Restaurant decoy steak and real-estate ordering are given as further examples.

Summary (11:02-11:30): defaults read as recommendations, the first number sets the anchor, a gift creates a debt, building something makes it yours, and progress, "even a fake one", creates momentum. The skill keeps the first four and rejects fake progress.

## GGg61sdEjeI: one product page, fifteen fixes and three upgrades

A fresh-strawberries product page.

1. Nav icons sit straight on the photo and vanish on a bright image; add a subtle container, contrast and a thin outline so they work on any photo (0:36-1:26). Design for the system, not one screen.
2. Product photo: of six options, the winner keeps the fruit natural on a clean background that every product in the catalog can share; a busy, hands-focused, single-berry (when sold by weight) or artificially coloured shot fails (2:00-3:32).
3. Alignment: one grid, consistent margins (24 px example) (3:32-4:17).
4. Colour: too bright and saturated, emphasis on the wrong things; switch to a softer palette that supports the product (4:17-4:53).
5. Typography: one family; hierarchy from size, weight, colour and line height (4:53-5:17).
6. Labels: small uppercase labels get letter spacing; the badge "20% off discount" plus icon becomes "20% off" (5:17-5:50).
7. Title: adjust size and weight so it stands out without shouting (5:50-6:18).
8. Paragraph: more line height, slightly lower contrast; headlines attract, paragraphs support (6:18-6:51).
9. Rating moves next to the title: what is it, and can I trust it (6:51-7:28).
10. Feature icons: one style, fewer colours, tighter spacing (8:18-8:58).
11. Divider lines: soften (8:58-9:20).
12. Spacing: too much space between sections breaks the relationship between them; tighten (9:20-9:44).
13. Remove the redundant "Price" label; if you label the price you would also have to label the quantity (9:44-10:14).
14. Title said "1 kg" while a quantity selector changes it; move quantity to the buying area. Move the price up near the title (10:14-10:55).
15. Add to cart: no all caps, less aggressive; put the quantity selector next to it with the unit inside; put the total inside the button (10:55-11:36).

Upgrades: content as a scrollable card that slides over the image, title moving into a sticky top bar (11:43-12:15); sticky bottom action area with quantity and add to cart (12:15-12:44); preset quantities from real purchase data (500 g, 1 kg, 2 kg) plus the custom selector (12:44-13:10).

## U7jTCgIWS4w: junior, mid, senior and staff versions of two screens

**Food delivery list (0:06-5:08).**
- Junior: food photos instead of logos (correct), but 56 px images, chosen to fit six restaurants above the fold. The presenter says above-the-fold cramming is outdated: everyone scrolls, so the first item must be worth continuing for (0:37-1:36). Filters and sort hidden in two dropdowns with 17 options (1:36-1:45). No hierarchy: 13 px name, everything else 11.5 px grey on one line; if you cannot say what is read first, users cannot either (1:45-1:58). 4 px corners next to circular category images: two shape languages (1:58-2:07).
- Mid: photos three times wider and the biggest element; one corner radius everywhere; better spacing and colour; hierarchy (name up, rating with star and weight, rest recedes) so the list can be scanned; eight visible filter chips (2:07-2:48).
- Senior: full-width photos; category row becomes a custom icon set so stock food photos stop competing with the meal photos below; a filter button and sort beside the quick filters; a count "640 places"; a cards/compact-list switch because no single default suits everyone (2:48-3:50).
- Staff: search placeholder names what you can search (places, dishes, cuisines); quieter icons; a "Best match" label on the top card from what is known about the user; name and numbers moved off the photo so the card works whatever photo a restaurant uploads (3:50-4:20). Caveat: none of the four is finished (4:20).

**Home search filters, 1,500+ homes in Portland (5:08-15:08).**
- Framing: filter requirements are soft. Three bedrooms might become two; a budget might stretch. A filter has to help people narrow while they are still deciding what they want (5:56-6:21).
- Results list (frames, about 5:28): applied filters show as filled chips ("2+ beds"), the count changes from "1,557 homes" to "48 homes", sort sits beside them. Listing cards carry an estimated monthly cost ("$624,000 · est. $4,450/mo") and price-cut badges.
- Junior: one full page per filter. Seven taps for two filters; each decision made blind to the others; results disappear while filtering; button "Show homes" gives no count, and the frames end on an empty results page (8:12-9:56). Looking simpler is different from making the decision easier. Its bedroom list already uses "at least" options (Any, Studio, 1+ to 4+), one page at a time.
- Frames around 9:48 contrast a "Simpler" booking card (price, "Book a viewing") with an "Easier" one that adds a "Fits your search" badge, the monthly cost, three time-slot chips, and a button that repeats the pick ("Book Sat 11:30").
- Mid: a sheet over the results with every filter in one scroll; price slider plus manual inputs; a button with a live count that changes on every adjustment (frames show 638, 480, 74, 41 and more; the narration quotes "Show 41 homes"). Criticism: steppers for bedrooms and bathrooms; steppers suit nudging a known value (basket quantity, thermostat), not picking one (9:56-11:27).
- Senior: a histogram of homes by price behind the slider, with a median context line in the frames ("3+ bed homes · median $568K"); every bedroom and bathroom option visible at once ("Studio, 1+ to 4+") plus an "Exact number of bedrooms" checkbox; icons for home type; a "No max" link beside the maximum price (11:27-12:57).
- Staff: the narration (13:11) credits the context line to this level; what the frames add is the match count "459 of 1,557". A mortgage helper, folded to one row ("Mortgage calculator · Up to … a month ›") until opened, turns deposit, comfortable monthly payment and rate into a shoppable price and draws it on the histogram ("Your reach · $708K"); helper copy reads "Add both and I'll work out the price you can shop at." The frames' collapsed summary ("Up to $10,550 a month") does not match the $5,000 entered, so do not reuse that number. Counts on each option before it is chosen ("3+ · 459", "4+ · 229"), recalculated from the other filters. Selecting two values makes a range: chips switch to exact values ("2 · 131", "3 · 231") with a "Clear" link for that section (12:57-15:01).
- Presenter's own caveat: more information makes the screen busier; keep what helps the decision and cut what is noise (15:01-15:08).

Frame-only detail in the food list: the junior's dropdown hides 17 options (time limits, price levels, minimum rating, six dietary options, free delivery); the mid shows them as a scrolling chip row with the selected chip filled; the senior keeps two chips with icons ("Under 30 min", "Free delivery") and moves the rest behind filter and sort buttons (about 1:38-3:20). A course slide at about 1:31 notes that device height, fixed navigation and text size move the fold, so the first view is designed by priority, not a pixel height. Another at about 4:30 shows a search field made to look like a control (icon, filled background, more height). The delivery app in the senior example is captioned "Global" but the frames show Glovo.

## How the videos present (frames)

Observed in the videos' own production, useful when demonstrating a before and after, not tested rules for product UI:

- Focus by dimming: the rest of the frame darkens and blurs; one element or a pill-shaped chapter card stays sharp.
- A cursor drives every interaction; labels arrive on leader lines naming the element ("HEADLINE", "HERO IMAGE").
- Copy edits happen in place on the button: "Subscribe" strikes out, "Start" types in.
- Timelines build node by node, each node popping before its caption fades up.
- Numbers are shown large and literal ("7 taps", "1,557 houses").
- Before and after sit side by side with a check mark on a connecting line, or A/B with large outline letters behind.
